CCentral Thesis — How to Assess a Century

Dermot Moran asks which achievements of twentieth-century philosophy still matter and which approaches have reached their limits. The century produced new methods and fields, but it also carried forward older questions. Its history cannot be told as the victory of one school over another. We need to trace continuities, changes, shared problems, and historical circumstances without imposing a misleading story of inevitable progress.

§IA Long Century: Philosophy and Its Changing Public

This section answers: Why was the century unusually difficult to assess?

Moran begins with Benedetto Croce's question about Hegel: what is living, and what is dead? Two world wars, totalitarian politics, genocide, scientific change, and globalization transformed the setting of philosophy. Meanwhile its results multiplied so quickly that a complete judgment of the period remained difficult.

Philosophy became mainly a university profession. Its growing specialization produced fields such as philosophy of language, mind and science, feminist philosophy, and environmental ethics. Women entered and changed the academic discipline, contributing not only to established subjects but also to new questions. Yet public reach and academic influence did not always coincide: political writers and engaged figures reached large audiences, while quieter researchers could alter philosophy's technical foundations.

§IINew Problems, Older Foundations

This section answers: What changed, and what survived from earlier philosophy?

Some advances were clearly new: modern formal logic, the philosophy of language, and more precise distinctions about thought and meaning. But philosophers continued to debate knowledge, time, consciousness, faith, and the limits of science. Naturalism expanded, while Kantian and phenomenological thinkers continued to ask how an objective world can be known by a subject. Even apparently new disciplines arose from nineteenth-century debates.

Why phenomenologists resisted naturalism

Moran notes that Husserl regarded naturalism as a danger to philosophy understood as a rigorous inquiry. The transcendental response asks about the knowing subject's contribution to the possibility of objectivity; it is not simply a denial of findings from natural science.

Dermot Moran, Towards an Assessment of Twentieth-Century Philosophy, p. 5.

The later opposition between analytic and Continental philosophy can obscure their shared origins. Both confronted German Idealism, the status of logic, the emergence of psychology, and the threat of relativism. Many early thinkers resisted psychologism, the attempt to explain logical truths merely through how human minds happen to think. Their disagreements concerned the methods needed to solve common problems.

Why the names themselves are difficult

Moran explains that 'Continental' was often a label applied from outside, especially in Britain. 'European philosophy' is also inadequate: many analytic philosophers were European, and Americans studied thinkers called Continental. Some historians even proposed 'Anglo-German' for analytic philosophy after German-speaking émigrés shaped it in the United States. The difficulty concerns both geography and overlapping methods.

Dermot Moran, Towards an Assessment of Twentieth-Century Philosophy, pp. 13–14.

★High-Yield — A Division Is Not Two Separate Origins

The labels analytic and Continental describe influential traditions, not sealed groups. Both deal with meaning, science, skepticism, and interpretation. Moran questions whether the labels can adequately describe an increasingly international discipline.

★A Nineteenth-Century Background That Did Not Disappear

German neo-Kantian philosophers reacted against Hegel, while British Idealists such as Bradley and McTaggart were still influential when Moore and Russell began their work. Later thinkers, including McDowell and Brandom, again drew on Hegel. Moran uses this return to show why a history of philosophy should not describe the defeat of idealism as an irreversible event.

§IIIFour Foundational Figures and the Turn to Language

This section answers: Why do Frege, Husserl, Wittgenstein, and Heidegger matter?

Around 1900, Freud's work, Husserl's Logical Investigations, Moore's early writings, and Russell's encounter with Peano marked different beginnings. Nietzsche also shaped later debates about truth, power, and history, although Moran distinguishes his broad cultural influence from his initial place in academic philosophy.

SOURCE DEPTHWhy Freud entered academic philosophy slowlyOptional original-source detail

Why Freud entered academic philosophy slowly

Although Freud's psychoanalysis helped shape twentieth-century culture, Moran reports that its direct impact on academic philosophers developed slowly. Sartre rejected a central Freudian claim, and Popper criticized psychoanalysis for its lack of falsifiability; later European thinkers returned to Freud in new ways.

Dermot Moran, Towards an Assessment of Twentieth-Century Philosophy, pp. 9–10.

Frege helped establish the precise logical treatment of statements and meanings. Husserl opposed psychologism and developed phenomenology as a description of what appears to consciousness. Wittgenstein and Heidegger became exceptionally influential, especially through their different turns toward language and interpretation. Moran also argues that Frege's and Husserl's contributions may be more foundational than familiar histories admit.

Husserl's method changed during its development

In the early Logical Investigations, Husserl described phenomenology in terms close to descriptive psychology, while rejecting the reduction of logic to psychology. His later work moved toward transcendental questions about how consciousness constitutes or reaches objective meaning. Moran treats both phases as important.

Dermot Moran, Towards an Assessment of Twentieth-Century Philosophy, pp. 10–11.

Frege's influence had limits, and a political shadow

Moran credits Frege with lasting influence on language, logic, science, mind and metaphysics, though not on every branch of philosophy. He also records Frege's association with a right-wing philosophical society that later supported Hitler and reports anti-Catholic and antisemitic statements in Frege's diary. These historical facts accompany his account of Frege's logical contributions.

Dermot Moran, Towards an Assessment of Twentieth-Century Philosophy, pp. 12–13.

§IVAnalytic Philosophy Revises Its Own Foundations

This section answers: How could analytic philosophy survive rejection of its early assumptions?

Russell initially described analysis as finding simpler elements within a complex claim. Frege and Russell distinguished a sentence's grammatical appearance from its logical form. The Vienna Circle later made scientific verification central and treated many metaphysical claims as meaningless. Yet analytic philosophy never had only one fixed program: Moore defended common sense, and ordinary-language philosophers studied how words work in daily use.

SOURCE DEPTHWhy Russell's ancestry is less straightforwardOptional original-source detail

Why Russell's ancestry is less straightforward

Moran reports that some historians portrayed Russell as an heir to Humean empiricism. He also cites Peter Hylton's finding that Russell was deeply shaped by Bradley's and McTaggart's idealism and continued to work on metaphysics. The usual labels can hide those earlier influences.

Dermot Moran, Towards an Assessment of Twentieth-Century Philosophy, pp. 28–29.

Quine attacked the analytic–synthetic distinction and argued that our beliefs meet experience as an interconnected body, not one statement at a time. Davidson then challenged the division between a conceptual scheme and the content it supposedly organizes. These revisions changed analytic philosophy without ending its continuing concern with conceptual clarity.

SOURCE DEPTHWhat Quine meant by challenging analytic truthOptional original-source detail

What Quine meant by challenging analytic truth

The analytic–synthetic distinction separates truths supposedly secured by meaning from those established by facts. Moran explains that Quine challenged the distinction itself, not merely a badly drawn boundary. He also rejected the idea that each claim could be checked against experience in isolation.

Dermot Moran, Towards an Assessment of Twentieth-Century Philosophy, pp. 18–19.

DThree Related Philosophical Critiques

Verificationism: a statement has factual meaning through a possible method of testing it. Holism: claims meet experience together with other claims, not always one at a time. Scheme/content division: the proposed separation between a framework of concepts and uninterpreted experience. Moran shows how Quine challenged the first two assumptions and Davidson challenged the third.

!Common Mistake — One Analytic Doctrine

Do not define the entire analytic tradition by logical positivism. Moran traces formal analysis, common-sense philosophy, ordinary-language work, and later criticism of empiricism inside that tradition.

§VWar, Migration, and the Question of Europe

This section answers: How did history change where and how philosophy was practiced?

For Moran, the two world wars had different effects. The First World War unsettled inherited intellectual habits and drew Russell into political opposition. The rise of Nazism and the Second World War caused devastating persecution, death, and the migration of philosophers, including members of the Vienna Circle and Frankfurt School. Academic philosophy's centre gradually moved from Europe toward the United States.

The meaning of Europe itself became a philosophical question: Husserl, Patočka, Derrida, and Habermas debated its intellectual inheritance and the limits of European identity. The historical movements of ideas also changed the labels assigned to thinkers. Political philosophy became prominent again through writers such as Arendt and, later, Rawls.

Why 'Europe' is not a neutral geographical label

Moran describes Husserl's attempt to locate a distinctive rational vocation in Europe while also noting criticism of his exclusions. Patočka questioned a post-European humanity, while Derrida and Habermas reconsidered Europe's philosophical inheritance in relation to political history and wider responsibilities.

Dermot Moran, Towards an Assessment of Twentieth-Century Philosophy, pp. 26–27.

Why political philosophy re-emerged later

Moran observes that systematic political philosophy developed surprisingly late in such a politically violent century. Arendt, Popper and Berlin contributed before Rawls's A Theory of Justice (1971). He also traces engaged critiques of colonialism and imperialism through Sartre, Merleau-Ponty and Fanon, alongside the Frankfurt School and later debates over emancipation.

Dermot Moran, Towards an Assessment of Twentieth-Century Philosophy, p. 31.

!History Must Not Erase Political Responsibility

Moran specifically recounts Heidegger's participation in Nazism and his treatment of his former teacher Husserl. He also records the persecution and deaths of philosophers, including Edith Stein and Walter Benjamin. These events are part of the philosophical history being assessed, not incidental dates outside it.

§VIAssessing Philosophy Without a Grand Narrative

This section answers: What kind of historical assessment does Moran finally recommend?

Moran warns against grand narratives that claim one method replaced all others or that philosophy has finally ended. Philosophers repeatedly announced new beginnings, yet their work kept returning to earlier problems. Philosophy must also examine its own history: ideas, names, and inherited problems do not have fixed meanings outside the debates that formed them.

SOURCE DEPTHWhy the history of philosophy is philosophicalOptional original-source detail

Why the history of philosophy is philosophical

Moran draws on Hegel's view that philosophy reflects on its own time, while resisting the idea that all events simply follow a rational master-plan. Concepts and problems have histories, and studying those histories can change how we understand the concepts themselves. A philosophical history is therefore more than a dated catalogue of works.

Dermot Moran, Towards an Assessment of Twentieth-Century Philosophy, pp. 24–25.

His discussion of identity and discrimination shows why philosophical classifications matter. To recognize a thing as something is also to distinguish it from other things; our ways of dividing the world therefore deserve scrutiny. Moran ends by preserving both the discovery of results and the ongoing activity of questioning as essential features of philosophy.

Chapter Summary

Moran asks what remains valuable and what has been left behind in twentieth-century philosophy. The century was exceptionally productive and turbulent: professional philosophy grew within universities, women transformed areas of research, new specialisms appeared, and logic and the study of meaning became more precise. Yet older debates about consciousness, time, knowledge, religion, and moral truth continued. Naturalism grew, but transcendental philosophy remained a powerful response to the challenge of explaining knowledge and normativity.

The century's apparently separate analytic and Continental traditions shared nineteenth-century problems, including the critique of psychologism and speculative idealism. Moran examines the different influence of Nietzsche, Freud, Husserl, Frege, Wittgenstein, and Heidegger, then traces analytic philosophy through Russell, logical positivism, ordinary-language philosophy, Quine, and Davidson. He also studies repeated declarations of philosophical endings, the challenges of writing philosophy's own history, and Europe's changing meaning. World wars, Nazi persecution, forced migration, and later developments in political philosophy profoundly reshaped the discipline. His final example of logical and social identity shows why one word can generate different, potentially connected inquiries. The hope for the twenty-first century is better appreciation of philosophical problems' many-sided character—not the assumption that all differences have already disappeared.

The claim that meaningful factual statements can each be tested separately by reducing them to immediate experience.

Quine's view that statements face experience together as a connected body of knowledge.

The proposed split between a framework that organizes experience and a separate, unorganized material to which it applies; challenged by Davidson.

Husserl's self-reflective philosophical inquiry, offered in response to a cultural crisis of meaning.

§IMoore's Challenge and Naturalistic Ethics

This section answers: Can the meaning of “good” be defined through a natural fact?

G. E. Moore objected to treating a description of good things as a definition of what “good” means. His open question argument asks: even if something gives pleasure, can we still meaningfully ask whether it is good? If so, “good” does not simply mean “gives pleasure.” Stout distinguishes knowing which things are good from explaining the meaning of the word.

SOURCE DEPTHWhy is Moore's question still open?Optional original-source detail

Why is Moore's question still open?

Stout warns that knowing how to use a word does not always mean that its meaning is perfectly clear to us. A question may look open even when it concerns meaning itself. Moore's test must therefore be applied carefully rather than treating every question that sounds discussable as proof that a definition fails.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, p. 854.

This did not end naturalistic ethics. John Dewey tied moral judgment to human experience without simply reducing goodness to pleasure. He proposed testing and revising moral principles through reflection and practice. Stout sees this as an early form of reflective equilibrium. Stout examines more reductive accounts, including Perry's treatment of value as an object of interest, as well as ideal-observer theories, relativism, and later Cornell realism. The central question remains whether a natural description fully captures a moral judgment.

What does revising a moral principle mean?

Stout distinguishes a weaker view, where describing and working through a situation shapes which moral questions arise, from a stronger view, where rational experimentation actually makes the outcome good. He is cautious about attributing this stronger anti-realist claim to Dewey himself.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, pp. 855–856.

Why imagine an ideal observer?

Roderick Firth proposed interpreting an ethical judgment through how a fully informed, disinterested, consistent observer would respond. Stout uses Moore's challenge to ask why that observer's reaction should settle what I ought to do. Brandt developed a related but differently qualified account.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, pp. 857–858.

What does it mean to value something?

Westermarck linked wrongness to a person's or community's inclination to disapprove. Perry linked an object's value to someone's taking an interest in it. Stout applies Moore's question to both: does disapproval establish wrongness, or does interest establish goodness? He also notes that Perry later appealed to harmony among interests when discussing overall value.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, p. 857.

Why is relativism included here?

Brandt connects ethical language to attitudes endorsed after informed, intelligent reflection. Harman interprets moral claims in relation to a community's framework of conventions and attitudes. Stout asks why an informed judge's endorsement, or acceptance within one's own framework, should settle whether an act is really right or wrong. That question remains open for the agent.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, pp. 858–859.

DDefinition — Extension and Meaning

Extension: the things to which a word applies. Intension: what the word means. Saying which things happen to be good does not automatically tell us what goodness means. Moore called the confusion between the two the naturalistic fallacy.

DMotivation and Moore's Historical Context

Humean internalism: a genuine moral judgment involves some motivation to act accordingly. It sharpens Moore's question: someone may recognize a proposed natural property yet ask, “Why should I care?” Stout also distinguishes Moore's attack on definitions of good from Quine's broader attack on analytic definitions.

!Common Mistake — All Naturalism Is Reduction

Stout distinguishes grounding ethical thought in human experience from defining away moral meaning as nothing but a natural fact. He does not treat Dewey and Perry as making the same claim.

§IINon-Cognitivism: Moral Language as Action

This section answers: What if moral statements express attitudes instead of describing facts?

Non-cognitivists argued that moral statements primarily express approval, disapproval, or instructions, rather than report facts that are true or false. Ogden and Richards introduced emotivism; Ayer and Stevenson developed it. For Richard Hare, a moral judgment is a prescription that must apply in every morally similar case. This universalizability distinguishes a moral command from an ordinary order.

Stout then presents problems for the theory. Two speakers can genuinely disagree about what should be done, not merely express different feelings. Peter Geach also asks how a moral statement can keep its meaning inside an “if … then” argument. Blackburn and Gibbard try to explain the logical force of such language while retaining an expressivist approach.

SOURCE DEPTHWhy does an if–then sentence matter?Optional original-source detail

Why does an if–then sentence matter?

Calling stealing wrong may seem like expressing disapproval. But in the statement 'If stealing is wrong, then …,' the speaker is not straightforwardly disapproving of stealing. Geach asks how the expression can still play the same logical role in an argument; Blackburn tried to answer through commitments between attitudes.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, p. 862.

If I say that smoking here is wrong and another person denies it, we appear to disagree about what ought to be done, not simply report two unrelated feelings. Stout argues that non-cognitivism struggles to explain why one person's reasons should also matter to another person. Hare's universalizability and later expressivist theories try to explain part of this difficulty without making ordinary moral claims straightforward reports of facts.

§IIIMoral Reality and the Possibility of Objectivity

This section answers: Does objective moral judgment require mysterious moral objects?

John Mackie's error theory argues that ordinary moral judgments claim an objective authority that the world does not provide. The Cornell realists answer that moral properties may be natural, causally important features of reality. John McDowell offers another path: moral judgment can depend on trained human sensibility yet be correct or incorrect independently of what a particular person happens to feel.

SOURCE DEPTH2 source details for this paragraphOptional original-source detail

What is an error theory?

Stout describes two parts of Mackie's challenge. Objective values would be unusual things in the world, and knowing them would require an equally unusual way of detecting them. Mackie therefore held that ordinary moral judgments claim more objectivity than exists and are, strictly speaking, false.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, p. 863.

What are the Cornell realists claiming?

Boyd described moral properties as clusters of mutually supporting natural properties, rather than definitions based on one feature. Sturgeon argued that someone's badness or goodness might help explain judgments and actions. Stout asks how the natural cluster connected with a moral term can be shown to coincide with what a person would reach through rational moral reflection.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, pp. 859–860.

Thomas Nagel argues that reasoning about one's own future interests can lead toward recognizing the interests of others as reasons to act. Christine Korsgaard connects moral authority with our reflective understanding of ourselves as agents. Stout thus shows several efforts to understand objectivity without simply returning to Moore's picture of a special non-natural property.

§IVIntuition, Virtue, and Moral Attention

This section answers: Must we infer every moral judgment from a universal rule?

Intuition here means judgment not inferred from a more basic rule; it need not mean a mysterious power. Sidgwick and Moore appealed to fundamental intuitive principles. W. D. Ross instead identified several prima facie duties, such as fidelity, gratitude, justice, and avoiding harm. These duties can conflict, leaving particular decisions less than certain.

SOURCE DEPTH3 source details for this paragraphOptional original-source detail

Why begin with intuitive principles?

Stout reports Sidgwick's movement from judgments about particular cases, to intuitive rules, to deeper philosophical principles that cannot themselves be derived from still deeper rules. Stout then asks why these final principles should escape further justification and finds Sidgwick's preference for maximizing good open to challenge.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, pp. 864–865.

How can duties conflict?

Ross named duties such as fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement, and avoiding harm. Their weight can differ by case, and consequences may be uncertain. Stout emphasizes that recognizing each duty intuitively does not mean that its final priority in a difficult situation is equally obvious.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, pp. 865–866.

What is a prima facie duty?

Stout connects Ross's approach to Beauchamp and Childress's four principles of medical ethics: autonomy, non-maleficence, beneficence and justice. They are distinct considerations that can guide decisions even when no single universal rule determines the answer. This is Stout's example of intuitionism's continuing practical influence.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, p. 866.

Later particularists and sensibility theorists, especially McDowell, stressed learning to notice what matters in a situation. Moral perception joins reason and appropriately developed emotion. Iris Murdoch described the task as careful attention to people and circumstances. Stout connects this approach with feminist care ethics, virtue theory, and Continental discussions of our responsibility to others.

SOURCE DEPTH2 source details for this paragraphOptional original-source detail

What is responsibility to others here?

Stout connects Murdoch's attentive moral perception with Buber's 'I–Thou' relation, where another person is not treated merely as an object. For Buber, responding to a particular person also bears on one's relation to God. Levinas develops an ethics of encountering the Other, while Scheler argues that emotional experience can disclose values. These are related approaches, not one identical theory.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, pp. 868–869.

What is a thick moral concept?

Stout reports Philippa Foot's example of the word 'rude'. A person's behavior may meet familiar criteria for being rude, while the word also expresses disapproval. Someone unwilling to make that evaluation may have to decline to use the term itself. This is why a morally thick word cannot be treated as a purely neutral description.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, p. 868.

For McDowell, virtue is not merely obeying a list or imitating a good person's actions. It is an acquired capacity to see what is morally relevant. This helps explain why Stout links moral knowledge, character, and emotion.

Gilligan's ethics of care seeks a response through relationships and a fresh description of the problem rather than only a contest between abstract duties. Thick concepts, such as “brutal” or “brave,” both describe and evaluate; thin concepts, such as “good,” say less about the situation.

Anscombe attacked moral systems built around general duties when their traditional source of authority was no longer accepted. MacIntyre examined how cultures understand virtues, without equating virtue with knowledge as McDowell does. Bernard Williams argued that ethical theories must respect an agent's particular commitments and integrity. Stout treats these as related developments, not a single doctrine.

§VAgency, Free Will, and Responsibility

This section answers: What makes an action genuinely ours and morally assessable?

Hume connected actions to beliefs and desires; Kant emphasized practical reason. Donald Davidson argued that an action's reasons can both make it understandable and cause it. Further disagreement concerned whether responsibility requires originating our own causes or having been able to do otherwise.

SOURCE DEPTHWhat does originating a cause mean?Optional original-source detail

What does originating a cause mean?

Stout reports Taylor's worry that describing mental events as causes leaves out the agent's own role in acting. Chisholm claimed free will requires some events to be caused by an agent rather than by prior events. Other philosophers judged this metaphysically costly, while compatibilists argued that determinism need not remove freedom or responsibility.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, pp. 870–871.

Harry Frankfurt offered a case where someone acts by choice even though a hidden intervener would have forced the same outcome if needed. P. F. Strawson grounded responsibility instead in human responses such as gratitude and resentment. Stout also traces an alternative: Charles Taylor sees self-definition as open to reasoned revision, while existentialists understand radical choice as a deeper source of freedom.

SOURCE DEPTHWhat is radical choice?Optional original-source detail

What is radical choice?

Stout contrasts Taylor's rationally revisable self-definition with existentialist radical choice, where value arises from a free decision rather than guiding it from outside. Simone de Beauvoir valued freedom but observed a tension: choosing freely gives a situation meaning, while that settled meaning also limits future freedom. She called this tension ambiguity.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, pp. 872–873.

DWilliams's Internal Reasons

Bernard Williams argues that a person's practical reason must connect to their existing motivational set of desires and other attitudes, even after reflection. Stout notes the later debate over whether all normative reasons, not just reasons that explain motivation, must have this connection.

§VIConsequences, Rules, Rights, and Agreements

This section answers: Which moral system should guide actual decisions?

Utilitarianism judges actions by their contribution to overall happiness or welfare. Stout traces a shift from pleasure toward preference satisfaction, which influenced decision theory and public policy. Nozick's experience-machine example questions whether pleasant experience is all we value. Singer extends moral consideration to sentient animals, while Parfit asks us to consider future generations.

SOURCE DEPTH4 source details for this paragraphOptional original-source detail

Why do future generations matter here?

Stout connects Parfit's concern for people not yet born with his view of personal identity. Our future selves, on this account, may be psychologically connected to us without being strictly the same person. That makes concern for future interests a wider question than simply protecting one's present self.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, p. 874.

Why do preferences matter to utilitarianism?

Ramsey showed how choices between outcomes could measure preferences and bets could estimate degrees of belief. This helped rational choice and later welfare calculations. Stout nevertheless questions treating preference-satisfaction as the whole of rationality: the fact that people choose what they want does not establish that whatever they want is what they ought to choose.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, pp. 873–874.

What does sentient mean in this argument?

Stout reports Singer's claim that all and only sentient beings count in overall-happiness calculations. On that principle, if a newborn has less capacity to feel pain than a cow, Singer gives more weight to the cow's well-being in that comparison. Stout also records Singer's qualification that other factors complicate the judgment in practice.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, p. 874.

What is Nozick's machine meant to test?

Imagine a machine that could give you every feeling of having achieved what matters to you. You could feel that you had written a great book without actually writing it. If you would refuse the machine, Nozick takes that to suggest that real achievements matter to you, not only the pleasant experience of having them.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, p. 873.

But good consequences can conflict with justice. Stout's utilitarian judge may have to release a known murderer to prevent deadly riots. Rules adopted for their usual benefits do not automatically settle that case. Hare distinguishes ordinary rule-following from critical calculation; rule-utilitarianism combines a generalization principle with a utilitarian way of selecting rules, though critics question whether it collapses into act-utilitarianism.

SOURCE DEPTH2 source details for this paragraphOptional original-source detail

Why are rules mentioned in utilitarianism?

Stout explains that Harrod combines two ideas: a Kantian-style reason to follow general rules, and a utilitarian method for deciding which rules would have the best consequences. Utility helps select the rules, but does not by itself explain why we must always act by rules.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, p. 876.

Why might rule-utilitarianism collapse?

Smart argued that the universally best rule might itself be 'always maximize utility in each case', making rule-utilitarianism collapse into act-utilitarianism. Lyons answered that some formulations escape this collapse, though they may be less plausible on other grounds. Stout presents this as a problem for justifying rules when immediate consequences point the other way.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, pp. 876–877.

Others tried to justify moral rules through self-interest, commitment, or rights. The Prisoners' Dilemma shows why self-interest alone can undermine cooperative rules. Judith Jarvis Thomson used rights to distinguish cases that have similar numerical outcomes. Joseph Raz connected rights to interests and duties. Scanlon, Rawls, and Habermas developed approaches grounded in agreements that reasonable people could accept.

SOURCE DEPTH3 source details for this paragraphOptional original-source detail

Why can cooperation fail?

Stout's problem is that everyone may be better off under shared rules, yet an individual can gain by breaking a rule while others obey it. If each person reasons that way, the system collapses. Gauthier responds by trying to build commitment into rational agency rather than treating every case as a fresh calculation.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, p. 877.

Why do agreements enter moral philosophy?

Stout distinguishes Scanlon's question about rules no one could reasonably reject, Rawls's imagined agreement behind a veil of ignorance about one's place in society, and Habermas's inclusive discussion in which participants respect each other's viewpoints. All use agreement, but not in the same way.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, p. 878.

Why move from self-interest to moral rules?

Baier argued that moral reasons take account of others and override immediate self-interest, while still grounding their priority partly in self-interest; Toulmin appealed instead to harmony among interests. Gauthier argued that rational agency includes commitment, which can sustain cooperation despite a temptation to cheat. Gewirth argued that claiming rights for oneself while refusing others' comparable rights is irrational.

Rowland Stout, Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy, p. 877.

DRights, Interests, and Duties

Rights can limit what may be done to one person for others' benefit. Stout contrasts cases in which a trolley is diverted from five people toward one with a surgeon killing one patient to supply organs to five others. Raz connects a person's right to interests significant enough to place another person under a duty; this does not make rights independent starting points in Raz's theory.

!Common Mistake — Rules and Consequences

Rule-utilitarianism does not merely mean “follow a rule whenever it is convenient.” It asks whether a rule-system's general acceptance would produce the best results. Stout then examines the difficulty of making that commitment genuinely binding.

✓Examination Tip — Follow the Judge Example

State what act-utilitarianism would recommend, explain why the result seems unjust, and then compare how rule-utilitarianism, rights, and contractual approaches address the problem. Do not treat all three as making identical arguments.

§VIIConclusion — A Debate That Crosses Traditions

This section answers: What overall development does Stout identify?

The chapter shows that ethical theories seldom stay within one of the five questions. Language leads to questions of truth; truth to moral knowledge; knowledge to motivation; and motivation to rules, rights, and political institutions. Stout portrays a century of continuing disputes but also of growing contact among Aristotelian, Humean, and Kantian approaches.

★Final Recall

Do not memorize the theories as isolated names. Follow the central connections: meaning → truth → knowledge → action → moral principles. A disagreement about one stage often changes how the others are understood.

Chapter Summary

Rowland Stout organizes twentieth-century moral philosophy around five overlapping questions: conceptual (what does moral language mean?), metaphysical (are there moral facts?), epistemological (how can we know or justify values?), moral psychological (how are judgments, motives and responsibility related?) and normative (what rules or principles should guide action?). He describes increasingly sophisticated work and some convergence among Aristotelian, Humean and Kantian traditions, while preserving their real disagreements. Moore challenges definitions of goodness; Dewey, other naturalists and Cornell realists explore the relation between natural experience and value; non-cognitivists reinterpret ethical language, meeting objections from the interpersonal open question and Geach's embedding problem. Mackie argues against objective values; Wiggins, McDowell, Nagel and Korsgaard offer different ways to understand moral truth, objectivity and reason. Sidgwick and Ross develop intuitionist theories, later connected with particularism, sensibility and virtue. Davidson, Frankfurt, Strawson, Sartre, Beauvoir and Taylor deepen questions about agency and freedom. Finally, utilitarians reconsider happiness, preferences, rules and moral scope, while rights theorists and contractualists examine justice and legitimate obligations. Stout's narrative moves into political philosophy rather than declaring that every ethical dispute has been settled.

CCentral Thesis

Philosophy of science is the systematic investigation into the validity of human knowledge and the nature of the reality science describes. It bifurcates into two inquiries: Epistemology (should we believe scientific claims, given the evidence?) and Metaphysics (what must the world be like, if those claims are true?). The field's central tension is the Problem of Induction — the gap between finite past observations and universal future laws — compounded by the challenge of Unobservability, where science posits entities (quarks, viruses) beyond direct human senses.

§IThe Epistemological Gateway: Induction and its Rivals

This section answers: What justifies believing a scientific law will keep holding tomorrow?

1. The Problem of Induction

Science seeks universal laws (e.g. "all sodium heated on a Bunsen burner glows orange"). But these laws are derived via Induction: moving from a finite number of past observations to a general conclusion about how nature will always behave.

  • The Logical Gap: unlike Deduction (premises guarantee the conclusion), induction offers no guarantee — there is no logical necessity that the future will resemble the past.
🦢
Memory Trick — ELI10

Imagine you've only ever seen white swans. You conclude "all swans are white." That's induction. The "Problem" is that the very next swan could be black, and your "law" would be shattered.

2. Popper's Falsificationism

Sir Karl Popper argued science does not actually use induction. Instead it uses Conjectures and Refutations: scientists propose a theory (conjecture) and then try to prove it wrong (falsification). A theory that survives is "undefeated," but never "proven."

DDefinition — The Demarcation Problem

Popper used falsifiability to distinguish science from pseudo-science (Marxism, Psychoanalysis, Astrology). Science makes precise predictions that could be wrong (Newtonian physics); pseudo-science is so vague that any outcome can be explained away (Astrology).

✕Common Mistake — Popper's Failing

Don't present Falsificationism as a flawless solution. Popper's theory struggles to explain "Positive Knowledge." We build bridges and cure diseases because we believe certain theories are right — not merely because they haven't yet been proven wrong. A purely falsificationist picture cannot easily account for this practical confidence.

3. Bayesianism (Degree of Belief)

Bayesians argue science is about subjective probability. Rationality means updating your "degree of belief" in a hypothesis as new evidence (E) arrives.

DBayes's Theorem

Prob(H/E) = Prob(H) × Prob(E/H) / Prob(E)

If evidence is surprising but predicted by your theory, your belief in that theory should increase significantly.

🎰
Memory Trick — The Dutch Book Argument

If your degrees of belief don't follow the rules of probability, a clever gambler could construct a set of bets against you such that you lose money regardless of the outcome. This is the classic justification for why rational belief should obey probability theory.

§IIThe Battle for Reality: Realism vs. Instrumentalism

This section answers: When science talks about electrons, is it describing something real, or just giving us a useful calculating tool?

1. The Realist Stance

Realists believe science provides an accurate description of the unobservable world (atoms, electrons).

★High-Yield Fact — The "No Miracles" Argument

Science lets us send people to the moon and predict novel phenomena. This would be a "miracle" if the underlying theories weren't at least approximately true — this is the classic Argument from Success for scientific realism.

2. The Instrumentalist Challenge

Instrumentalists view theories as "calculating tools" rather than literal truths.

  • Underdetermination of Theory (UTD): based on the Duhem–Quine Thesis — for any set of data, multiple incompatible theories could fit it. We can always "save" a theory by changing auxiliary hypotheses.
  • Pessimistic Meta-induction: history shows most past theories (Phlogiston, Indivisible Atoms) were eventually proven false. Therefore our current theories are probably false too.

3. Theory-Dependence and Incommensurability

Kuhn and Feyerabend argued that even "seeing" is theoretical.

👁️
Clarifying Example — The Müller–Lyer Illusion

Two lines of identical length appear different because of arrow-shaped fins at their ends. This is a visual proof that our sensory systems already use "theories" about the world to interpret raw data — undermining the idea of a neutral, theory-free observation.

DDefinition — Incommensurability

There is no neutral "yardstick" to compare two scientific theories/paradigms, because the very language of observation changes when the theory changes.

Modern metaphysics of causation began with David Hume's Constant Conjunction: we never actually see a "cause," only one event consistently following another.

Not all regularities are laws. "All the screws in my desk are copper" is a true regularity, but not a law of nature.

§IVQuantum Mechanics and Biological Purpose

This section answers: Do the special sciences (quantum physics, biology) break the tidy epistemic/metaphysical picture built above?
DDefinition — The Measurement Problem

On the orthodox view of Quantum Mechanics, the "wave function" (a spread of probabilities) only collapses into a determinate reality when a measurement is made. This leads to the famous Schrödinger's Cat paradox: is the cat both dead and alive until we look?

Teleology in Biology

We explain the polar bear's fur by its future purpose (camouflage). Modern philosophers see this as a "disguised" causal explanation: past survival advantage (natural selection) caused the trait to persist — the "purpose" language is shorthand for a causal history, not literal forward-looking design.

Reductionism

The idea that biology reduces to chemistry, and chemistry to physics.

DDefinition — Token-Identity

Every particular biological event is a physical event, but there may be no general physical law that covers all instances of, say, "aggressive behavior" across every different species.

!Exam Tip

If asked to evaluate teleological explanation in biology, don't simply say "it's outdated." The stronger answer notes that teleological language survives in modern biology as a convenient shorthand for a genuinely causal, backward-looking selection story — it has been reinterpreted, not eliminated.

§VConclusion

The investigation of science moves from the Epistemological (justification of induction; realism vs. instrumentalism) to the Metaphysical (causation, laws, probability). Science is not a simple collection of facts but a complex structure of conjectures navigating the limits of observation and the logical gaps of induction. Whether science describes a literal reality (Realism) or acts as a highly effective map (Instrumentalism), it remains the most successful tool for navigating the natural world.

"A single contrary example suffices for a conclusive disproof, but no number of supporting examples will constitute a conclusive proof."

Sartre, Foucault and Derrida

From radical freedom, to power/knowledge, to the instability of language

In This Chapter — Three Movements

  1. The Sovereignty of the Subject (Sartre) — Being-for-itself vs. Being-in-itself; freedom as negation.
  2. The Archeology of Power (Foucault) — from the mind to the "episteme"; madness, criminality, and surveillance.
  3. The Instability of Language (Derrida) — dismantling binary oppositions; différance; the impossibility of pure presence.
"Nothingness must, therefore, exist like 'a worm in the heart of being'." — Jean-Paul Sartre
CCentral Thesis

Late twentieth-century French philosophy pivots from Existential Phenomenology, which grounds the world in human consciousness and radical freedom, to Poststructuralism, which de-centres the human subject in favour of the underlying structures of history, power, and language. While Sartre viewed the individual as the absolute source of meaning, Foucault and Derrida challenged this "subjective" dominance: Foucault revealed identity as a contingent product of historical power regimes; Derrida showed that Western thought's own logic is built on unstable linguistic oppositions that perpetually "deconstruct" themselves.

§IJean-Paul Sartre: The Philosopher of Radical Freedom

This section answers: If consciousness has no fixed nature, what makes us free — and why do most people flee from that freedom?

Sartre's philosophy, primarily detailed in Being and Nothingness, is built on the nature of consciousness and its relationship to the world.

1. The Nature of Consciousness (Being-for-itself)

Sartre adopts Husserl's concept of Intentionality: consciousness is always of something. But Sartre adds a radical twist: consciousness itself is nothing.

  • Transparency: consciousness has no "inside," no soul, no spiritual substance — its entire existence is exhausted by its relation to the objects it perceives.
  • Being-for-itself: unlike a rock, consciousness is self-aware; this internal relation to itself makes it "for-itself."
  • Being-in-itself: the world of objects — "brute, unstructured givens" that are "superfluous" and "absurd," with no intrinsic meaning until consciousness interprets them.

2. Nihilation and the "Worm in Being"

How does meaning enter the world? Through Nihilation (néantisation).

☕
Clarifying Example — The Café

If you are looking for Pierre in a café and he is not there, you do not simply see a room — you see a "not-Pierre" café. You have introduced nothingness into the scene, just by expecting and failing to find him.

DDefinition — Freedom as Withdrawal

Sartre identifies the ability to say "no" or "not" with Freedom. Freedom is the "withdrawal of consciousness from being." Because we are not "things" (Being-in-itself), we are not bound by causal laws. We are, in his phrase, "condemned to be free."

3. Bad Faith and Authenticity

Most humans flee from the "anguish" of absolute freedom.

  • Bad Faith (Mauvaise foi): lying to oneself by pretending one is a "thing" with a fixed nature — e.g. "I can't help it, that's just my personality."
  • Authenticity: the ethical goal — accepting one's status as radically free, and recognising oneself as the "ultimate source of all meaning."

§IIMichel Foucault: The Historian of Systems

This section answers: If Sartre says the individual creates meaning, what happens when we discover the "rules of the game" were already there before we were born?

Foucault represents the "poststructuralist reaction." He rejects Sartre's focus on the individual subject, looking instead at the "objective structures" that shape human experience.

1. Archeology and the Episteme

Foucault uses Archeology to uncover the "epistemic unconscious" — the hidden rules of a time period that dictate what can be said and what counts as truth. In The Order of Things, he argues disciplines like biology or linguistics are not "progressing" toward truth, but merely shifting from one epistemic structure to another.

2. The History of Madness

Foucault identifies two major "breaks" in how Europe viewed madness:

Renaissance
Madness was an alternative mode of human existence — a different kind of "reason"
Classical Age (17th c.)
Madness redefined as "Unreason"; the mad were physically excluded (confinement), seen as having "plunged into animality"
Modern Age (19th c.+)
The mad are brought back into society but treated as "mental illness"; the asylum was not a humanitarian gift but a "gigantic moral imprisonment" designed to control the mad through guilt

3. Power, Knowledge, and the Body

In Discipline and Punish, Foucault introduces Genealogy (influenced by Nietzsche).

DDefinition — Power/Knowledge

Knowledge is not a "pure" pursuit; it is inextricably tied to power. Power generates knowledge about the people it wants to control, and that knowledge, in turn, increases power.

  • Surveillance: modern society moves from the "spectacle" of public execution to a "society of surveillance" — control exercised by monitoring bodies in schools, factories, prisons.
  • The Self-Scrutinizing Subject: in History of Sexuality, Foucault notes we are now controlled by our own knowledge of ourselves. Trying to find our "true" sexual nature, we merely conform to norms laid down by scientific disciplines.

Memory Trick — ELI10

Imagine you are playing a game. Sartre says you are the one who decides the rules of the game. Foucault says the game board, the pieces, and the rules were already there before you were born, and they actually decide who you are allowed to be.

!Exam Tip

When explaining Power/Knowledge, resist reducing it to "knowledge is used by the powerful." Foucault's stronger and more examinable claim is that power and knowledge are mutually constitutive — power doesn't just use pre-existing knowledge, it actively produces the categories (madness, sexuality, criminality) that knowledge then studies.

§IIIJacques Derrida: The Reader of Textual Limits

This section answers: If Foucault shows the mind is shaped by external structures, what happens when we discover that language — the very tool we think with — cannot deliver the fixed truths philosophy has always wanted?

Derrida constantly returns to the "body of failed philosophical knowledge" to dissect it.

1. Logocentrism and Deconstruction

Western philosophy is Logocentric: obsessed with finding a "pure presence" or absolute truth (God, The Good, The Self). This rests on Binary Oppositions:

  • Presence vs. Absence
  • Truth vs. Falsity
  • Speech vs. Writing (Speech usually prioritized as "purer")
DDefinition — Deconstruction

The technique of showing that binary oppositions are unstable. The "lower" term (e.g. writing) is often actually necessary for the "higher" term (e.g. speech) to exist at all.

2. Différance: Difference and Deferral

Derrida coined the neologism différance (with an "a") to describe how meaning works. It carries two meanings simultaneously:

  • To Differ: a sign (word) only has meaning because it differs from other signs.
  • To Defer: meaning is never fully "present" right now — it is always put off until the next sign, and the next.
★High-Yield Fact — Pattern Recognition

The "a" in différance is visible in writing but silent in speech. This subverts the very "priority of speech" that Logocentrism assumes — because writing can express a distinction that speech cannot. The pun is the argument.

3. Ethics and Justice

For Derrida, Justice is not the same as Law.

  • Law: a system of rules that is stable and can be deconstructed.
  • Justice: an infinite demand that can never be fully satisfied by any law.

Deciding what is just in a specific moment requires a "leap" or a "madness," because the law can never fully tell you what is right in a unique situation.

§IVConclusion — From the Internal to the External

The journey from Sartre to Derrida moves from the Internal to the External. Sartre finds the "nothingness" inside the human mind that allows for absolute freedom. Foucault finds that this "mind" is actually constructed by external historical "epistemes" and power structures. Derrida finalises this by showing that even our language — the tool we use to think — is a system of shifting differences that can never capture absolute truth.

Continental Philosophy · The Meaning Crisis
1.1 · 20th-Century PhilosophySource: Continental Philosophy↑ Contents

The Gap Between Knowledge and Wisdom

Why more facts have not brought more meaning

In This Chapter — The Historical & Conceptual "Split"

  1. The Ancient Foundation — a unified circle where knowledge and wisdom were one.
  2. The 17th-Century Fracture — modern science demands empirical proof; philosophy becomes an "under-labourer."
  3. The Modern Paradox — as knowledge increases, the "felt gap" of meaning grows more acute.
  4. The Continental Bridge — thinkers who try to reconnect rigorous truth with the "drama of life."
"The eternal silence of infinite spaces fills me with dread." — Blaise Pascal
CCentral Thesis

The defining crisis of modern thought is the widening "meaning gap" between Knowledge (scientia) — the empirical explanation of how the world functions — and Wisdom — the reflective understanding of what it means to lead a good human life. Ancient philosophy integrated these two realms; the post-17th-century scientific revolution relegated wisdom to the fringes, leaving professional philosophy as a mere "under-labourer" to science. Continental philosophy emerges as the primary attempt to bridge this abyss, insisting that philosophy, to remain valid, must tackle the existential question of the meaning of life.

§IThe Ancient Ideal: Philosophy as Formation

This section answers: What did philosophy look like before knowledge and wisdom were pulled apart?

In the ancient world, philosophy was defined as the "love of wisdom." It was not merely an academic exercise but a reflective practice aimed at "forming" human beings rather than just "informing" them.

  • The Examined Life: for Socrates and his successors, "the unexamined life is not worth living." The reverse is also true: "the unlived life is not worth examining" — ancient philosophy was inseparable from the polis, the public realm of political and social life.
  • Teleological Universe: the ancients held a "teleological view," where the cosmos was believed to express human purpose.
DDefinition — Final Cause (Aristotle)

The idea that every natural thing has a goal or purpose for its existence. In this view, nature is a "living book" where theory (understanding) and practice (living) are harmonised.

§IIThe Modern Shift: From Wisdom to Scientia

This section answers: What did the Scientific Revolution actually do to philosophy's job description?

Since the 17th century, philosophy has undergone a radical transformation, moving from a practical pursuit of the "good life" to a theoretical inquiry into the conditions of knowledge.

  • The Rise of Epistemology: modern philosophy is dominated by the question "How do we know what we know?" — it becomes the "theory of knowledge."
DDefinition — The "Under-Labourer" Status

John Locke famously described the philosopher's role not as a leader, but as a "janitor" in the "Crystal Palace of the sciences" — tasked with clearing away the rubbish that blocks scientific progress.

  • Knowledge (Scientia): claims must be verified by experiment (e.g. the chemical makeup of water) or they are rejected.
  • Faith vs. Fact: statements about meaning or divinity (e.g. "Jesus is the redeemer") are relegated to faith because they lack empirical proof.

§IIIThe Paradox of Progress and the "Meaning Gap"

This section answers: Why does more scientific progress seem to produce more, not less, existential anxiety?

A "massive paradox": as society becomes more technologically advanced and scientifically "enlightened," the sense of meaninglessness actually increases.

  • Disenchantment of Nature (Max Weber's term): nature is no longer a "world soul" but "sheer, impersonal objective 'stuff'" governed by indifferent physical laws.
  • The Appendix of Wisdom: wisdom is now treated like a vestigial organ — an "appendix we believed we no longer needed," yet it continues to "irritate" us.
  • Luxury and Neurosis: borrowing from Bertolt Brecht ("food first, then ethics"), when humans are "showered with every earthly blessing," they concoct new miseries. The question "What is the point of my life?" returns with a "frightening vengeance."

Chapter Summary

Ancient philosophy fused knowledge and wisdom into a single teleological worldview, where understanding nature's purposes and understanding how to live were the same project. The 17th-century Scientific Revolution severed this unity: philosophy was demoted to Locke's "under-labourer," clearing conceptual rubbish for science, while questions of meaning were exiled to faith. The resulting paradox is that scientific and material progress has not closed the "meaning gap" but widened it — Weber's "disenchantment" strips nature of soul, and comfort itself seems to provoke new existential neuroses. Mill's warning identifies two equally dangerous escape routes — Scientism (reducing humans to beasts) and Obscurantism (reducing humans to lunatics) — while Continental philosophy, from Hegel through Camus, offers the only sustained attempt to take the question of meaning seriously without collapsing into either extreme.

Key Definitions

Scientia
Knowledge in the empirical, scientific sense — verifiable claims about how the world functions.
Final Cause
Aristotle's idea that natural things exist for a purpose or goal.
Disenchantment
Weber's term for nature losing its "world soul" status under scientific rationalisation.
Scientism
The view that all meaningful questions must be answered by empirical science.
Obscurantism
Rejecting scientific rigour in favour of esoteric or pseudo-spiritual belief systems.

Possible Examination Questions

Short Answer
  1. What does Locke mean by describing philosophers as "janitors in the Crystal Palace of the sciences"?
  2. Define Scientism and Obscurantism, and explain why Mill treats them as parallel dangers.
Long Answer
  1. Explain how the ancient concept of teleology unified knowledge and wisdom, and how the Scientific Revolution broke that unity.
  2. Using at least three named thinkers, explain how Continental philosophy attempts to bridge the "meaning gap."
Comparison
  1. Compare the Ancient, Modern/Analytic, and Continental views of the role of the philosopher and the risk each tradition faces.
Conceptual
  1. What is the "paradox of progress," and why does material comfort seem to intensify rather than resolve the meaning gap?
Application
  1. A wealthy, materially comfortable society reports rising rates of reported meaninglessness. Using Weber's "disenchantment" and Brecht's "food first, then ethics," explain this apparent paradox.
